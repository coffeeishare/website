<SectionTitle eyebrow="01 — Problem" label="The site was the weakest part of the product story" />

Companion had grown significantly as a product. The website hadn't kept up. Session recordings showed drop-off concentrated at call-to-action points. Accessibility audits flagged contrast failures and semantic structure gaps. The messaging on the homepage described a version of the product that no longer existed.

<EvidenceQuote
  skill="strategy"
  text="The product's great but the site made it feel outdated. Your competition seemed to make more effort so I looked into their offering first."
/>

The internal challenge wasn't design — it was buy-in. Stakeholders had limited appetite for a full redesign without a business case. My first task was framing the project as a growth lever backed by data, not a visual refresh driven by preference.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="02 — Research" label="Heuristics, analytics, and stakeholder interviews" />

The discovery was desk-based and stakeholder-led rather than user interview-driven. I structured it across three inputs: a heuristic evaluation against conversion and accessibility standards, a session recording and analytics review to pinpoint drop-off, and a round of stakeholder interviews to surface where internal perception of the site had drifted from reality.

The SEO opportunity was a secondary finding from the analytics review. Organic search traffic had flatlined while competitor sites were indexed more broadly. Better heading structure and more specific landing page copy were quick wins that could compound the redesign's impact without additional effort.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="03 — Strategy" label="A growth lever, not a visual refresh" />

The framing that got stakeholder buy-in was simple: the site was leaking conversion, and there was a clear, evidence-backed path to fix it. I connected the session drop-off data to estimated revenue impact and proposed the redesign as a growth initiative with measurable outcomes — not a design opinion.

<EvidenceQuote
  skill="strategy"
  text="I framed the redesign as a growth lever backed by data — not a visual refresh. That framing was what got stakeholder buy-in despite limited resources."
/>

The second strategic decision was to introduce a shared token system during the project. Companion's site had been maintained through inconsistent one-off changes, with no shared design language for colours, typography, or spacing. Introducing a token system meant future updates would be faster and more consistent — without requiring a redesign each time. This was the kind of compounding investment that was easy to justify alongside a full redesign but hard to prioritise standalone.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="04 — Design decisions" label="Messaging first, aesthetics second" />

**Lead with what changed.** The homepage structure was reorganised to front-load the product's current differentiators — AI-driven discovery, campaign analytics, creator data — rather than generic category claims. Each section was written to answer a specific question a prospective buyer would have, in the order they would have it.

<ProjectImage src="/images/companion-website/component-headers.webp" alt="Redesigned header sections leading with product differentiators" style={{width: '100%', borderRadius: '12px', margin: '1.5rem 0'}} />

**CTA clarity over CTA frequency.** The original site had multiple competing calls to action. The redesign collapsed these to two: a primary CTA for trial sign-up and a secondary one for a demo. Removing ambiguity at decision points was the simplest intervention for the drop-off pattern the data had identified.

**Accessibility as baseline.** All contrast values were corrected to WCAG AA minimum. Heading hierarchy was rebuilt from the ground up — both for screen reader usability and for SEO indexing benefit. These were non-negotiable from a compliance standpoint and compounded the traffic outcome.

**Token system.** Colour, typography, and spacing were extracted into a shared token set, documented in Figma, and handed off with the component library. This meant development could be faster and more consistent — and any future copy or visual updates could be made without touching the design architecture.
