<SectionTitle eyebrow="01 — Problem" label="Breadth is the pitch and the blocker" />

Tulip's value proposition is flexibility: it can digitise almost any workflow on a manufacturing floor, from quality checks to operator training to production tracking. In sales conversations, that flexibility works against the product. New prospects don't want infinite possibility — they want to see their specific process working. Without a fast path to a concrete example, demos stalled.

<EvidenceQuote
  skill="strategy"
  text="Tulip can digitise almost any process on the shop floor. But when you're sitting across from someone who has never seen the platform, that breadth is exactly what makes it hard to explain."
/>

The secondary problem was onboarding. Customers who signed — sold on the platform's power — hit the same blank-canvas friction when they tried to build their first app independently. The sales problem and the onboarding problem were the same problem: Tulip needed a faster path to value for someone who had never built anything on the platform.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="02 — Research" label="Shadowing demos and interviewing customers post-purchase" />

We shadowed sales demos and interviewed customers who had already purchased but were struggling to get started independently.

Three themes surfaced across every session:

  skill="user-research"

  notes={[
    "Demo is static — nothing adapts to a customer's actual process",

The pattern was the same everywhere: the problem wasn't capability. It was legibility. Customers needed a concrete starting point — something specific to their process, fast.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="03 — Strategy" label="From website feature to core sales tool" />

The project started with a narrow brief. It expanded once we understood where conversations were actually breaking down.

<EvidenceQuote
  skill="strategy"
  text="The scope shifted from a website feature to a core sales tool once we saw where conversations were actually stalling."
/>

The strategic reframe was to treat Operations Composer as an accelerator for the sales and onboarding motion, not just a product feature. That changed the success criteria: instead of measuring feature adoption among existing users, we measured how quickly a new prospect could get to a working app in a guided session. That's a different bar — and it drove fundamentally different design decisions about what to show first, how much to guide vs. allow freeform exploration, and where to hand off to the human in the room.

The AI role was positioned as a guide, not an automator. The product couldn't — and shouldn't — build the customer's app for them. But it could take a natural language description of a process and propose a realistic starting structure that the customer could react to, modify, and own. Getting to a concrete reaction in under five minutes was the goal.

<SectionTitle eyebrow="04 — Design decisions" label="Opinionated starting point, full editorial control" />

**Process description to structure, immediately.** The entry point was a plain-text field asking: "What process do you want to digitise?" The AI generated a proposed app structure directly from that input — steps, fields, logic — in a matter of seconds. Getting to a concrete artefact fast was the priority. Users who had a starting point to react to moved faster and felt more confident than users presented with a blank canvas.

**Editable at every level.** Every generated element was immediately editable. Steps could be renamed, reordered, or deleted. Fields could be swapped. The AI's proposal was treated as a draft, not a specification. This was the same principle established in AI Composer: the mental model shift from "I'm trusting a machine" to "I'm reviewing a draft" was critical to building user confidence.

**Demo mode as first-class surface.** Given the sales use case, we designed the experience to work well in a live conversation — with a sales engineer driving and a prospect watching. That meant clear progressive disclosure (nothing overwhelming upfront), legible loading and generation states, and a result that looked polished enough to anchor a conversation.

**Figma Make prototype for buy-in.** Before any engineering investment, I built an interactive prototype in Figma Make that simulated the end-to-end flow — including the generation interaction, editing states, and loading behaviour. This was the artefact used to secure internal buy-in and run initial concept testing with the commercial team.
